(8) who is subject to a court order that-

(A) was issued after a hearing of which such person received actual notice, and at which such person had an opportunity to participate;

(C)(i) includes a finding that such person represents a credible threat to the physical safety of such intimate partner or child; or

United States v. Rahimi, 602 U.S. 680 (2024)

Supreme Court of the United States · 2024-06-21 · 22-915 published
Basis (a): decided on or after the current version

Summary (non-official) : The Court held that 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(8) is consistent with the Second Amendment when applied to an individual subject to a qualifying domestic-violence restraining order. An individual found by a court to pose a credible threat to another’s physical safety may therefore be temporarily disarmed.

Held: When an individual has been found by a court to pose a credible threat to the physical safety of another, that individual may be temporarily disarmed consistent with the Second Amendment. Pp. 5–17.
Together, the surety and going armed laws confirm what common sense suggests: When an individual poses a clear threat of physical violence to another, the threatening individual may be disarmed. Section 922(g)(8) is not identical to these founding-era regimes, but it does not need to be. Like the surety and going armed laws, Section 922(g)(8)(C)(i) applies to individuals found by a court to threaten the physical safety of another.

Summary (non-official) : The court held that § 922(g)(8) requires the protected person to be an intimate partner, but does not require the state protection order to expressly designate that person as an intimate partner. The order’s omission of that designation therefore did not prevent prosecution under § 922(g)(8) or violate due process notice requirements.

An Iowa state court issued a protection order against Justin Dwight SholleyGonzalez. In issuing the order, the court did not expressly designate the protected person as an “intimate partner” under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(8), though the protected person was in fact an intimate partner. Under § 922(g)(8), a person subject to a court order protecting an intimate partner is restricted from possessing firearms and ammunition.

United States v. Miller, 646 F.3d 1128 (8th Cir. 2011)

United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit · 2011-07-28 · 10-3475 published
Basis (a): decided on or after the current version

Summary (non-official) : The court held that § 922(g)(8) requires the government to prove that the defendant knowingly possessed a firearm while subject to a qualifying restraining order, but does not require proof that he knew such possession was unlawful. Given the protective order’s terms and his status as an intimate partner, Miller lacked a viable due-process or fair-warning defense based on ignorance of the federal prohibition.

Section 922(g)(8) renders unlawful the possession of a firearm by an individual subject to certain restraining orders. Section 924(a)(2) provides the penalties for those who “knowingly” violate § 922(g). ‘“[Kjnowingly’ supplies the mens rea for the conduct proscribed in section 922 as designated in section 924. The statutes do not require knowledge of the law nor an intent to violate it.” United States v. Farrell, 69 F.3d 891, 893 (8th Cir.1995). The penalty provisions of § 924(a)(2) thus require the government to prove that the defendant knew of the facts that constituted the offense under § 922(g), not that the defendant knew that his possession of a firearm was illegal.
