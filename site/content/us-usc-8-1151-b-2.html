8 U.S.C. § 1151(b)(2) — Worldwide level of immigration — (b)(2)

in forceIn force since : 2009-10-28 · Applicable : US

Official source : https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1151&num=0&edition=prelim · govinfo (USCODE) · Interactive browser

Current text

(2)(A)(i) Immediate relatives.-For purposes of this subsection, the term "immediate relatives" means the children, spouses, and parents of a citizen of the United States, except that, in the case of parents, such citizens shall be at least 21 years of age. In the case of an alien who was the spouse of a citizen of the United States and was not legally separated from the citizen at the time of the citizen's death, the alien (and each child of the alien) shall be considered, for purposes of this subsection, to remain an immediate relative after the date of the citizen's death but only if the spouse files a petition under section 1154(a)(1)(A)(ii) of this title within 2 years after such date and only until the date the spouse remarries. For purposes of this clause, an alien who has filed a petition under clause (iii) or (iv) of section 1154(a)(1)(A) of this title remains an immediate relative in the event that the United States citizen spouse or parent loses United States citizenship on account of the abuse.

(ii) Aliens admitted under section 1181(a) of this title on the basis of a prior issuance of a visa to their accompanying parent who is such an immediate relative.

(B) Aliens born to an alien lawfully admitted for permanent residence during a temporary visit abroad.

Taylor v. Barnhart, 399 F.3d 891 (8th Cir. 2005)

United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit · 2005-03-03 · 04-1132 published
Basis (b): identical text — functional-review

Justification : The decision interpreted 8 U.S.C. § 1151(b)(2), particularly the definition of an “immediate relative” and the treatment of a child admitted on that basis. The 2009 amendment removed a two-year marriage-duration requirement applicable to surviving spouses in § 1151(b)(2)(A)(i), not the child/immediate-relative language relevant to Taylor, so the interpreted rule remains functionally identical.

Summary (non-official) : The court treated Taylor’s admission as an immediate relative of a U.S. citizen under 8 U.S.C. § 1151(b) as potentially establishing a basis for lawful presence and Social Security eligibility. It held that the record was insufficient to determine whether her immediate-relative petition remained valid or was later converted, so mandamus relief was unavailable.

This definition of an alien “lawfully present in the United States” provides a potential avenue of relief for Taylor. Taylor was inspected and admitted to the United States in 1984 as an immediate relative of a citizen of the United States. See 8 U.S.C. § 1151(b). The district court thought that her adoptive father’s petition for immediate relative status was automatically revoked when Taylor reached age 21, pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 205.1(a)(3)(i)(F), but the record does not appear to support that conclusion.

Basis (b): identical text — functional-review

Justification : The decision interpreted 8 U.S.C. § 1151(b)(2)(A)(i) insofar as a U.S. citizen’s spouse may qualify as an “immediate relative,” and addressed the agency’s discretion in evaluating that classification. The 2009 amendment altered only the separate surviving-spouse provision in the second sentence of clause (i), not the spouse definition applied in Gipson, so the interpreted language remains functionally identical.

Summary (non-official) : The court held that classification of an alien spouse as an immediate relative is discretionary and may be denied when the agency lacks reliable information about the applicant’s identity, origin, and family ties. A valid marriage license alone does not require approval of the immediate-relative petition.

INS, 798 F.2d 1189, 1191 (8th Cir.1986) (reviewing decision denying non-immigrant treaty investor status); see also Egan v. Feldman, 722 F.2d 893, 898 (1st Cir.1983) (reviewing denial of petition to have alien classified as sixth-preference -immigrant on basis of profession).
A United States citizen can petition to have an alien spouse classified as an “immediate relative,” a preferential immigration classification. 8 U.S.C. § 1151(b)(2)(A)(i). Immediate relatives may be admitted to the United States without numerical limitations. 8 U.S.C. § 1151(b). Once an alien’s petition for identification as an immediate relative is approved, he or she may then petition for an adjustment of status to that of a person admitted for permanent residence. 8 C.F.R. § 245 et seq. Sabhari v. Reno, 197 F.3d 938 (8th Cir. 1999)

United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit · 1999-11-26 · 98-3843 published
Basis (b): identical text — functional-review

Justification : Sabhari interpreted 8 U.S.C. § 1151(b)(2)(A)(i)’s definition of an immediate relative to include the spouse of a United States citizen, together with the anti-fraud provision in § 1154(c). The later 2000 and 2009 amendments changed other language in § 1151(b)(2)(A)(i), but did not alter the spouse-definition language applied in the decision, so its wording and function remain consistent.

Summary (non-official) : The court held that a United States citizen may petition to classify an alien spouse as an immediate relative under 8 U.S.C. § 1151(b)(2)(A)(i), but that the petition must be denied under § 1154(c) when the marriage was entered into to evade the immigration laws. The court upheld review of the agency decision and addressed whether substantial evidence supported the marriage-fraud determination.

When a United States citizen marries an alien, the citizen may petition the INS to have his or her spouse classified as an immediate relative. See 8 U.S.C. § 1154 (establishing petitioning procedure) and § 1151(b)(2)(A)(i) (defining immediate relative to include citizen’s spouse). Once so classified, the alien may then apply under a separate procedure for an adjustment of immigration status — a process which will upgrade the alien to the status of lawful permanent resident of the United States.
Approval of the alien’s petition for adjustment of status is not a foregone conclusion, however, and it has long been recognized that the INS may combat fraud by refusing to confer benefits where a marriage has been entered so as to circumvent the immigration laws.
