Summary rule

Burnham v. Superior Court, 495 U.S. 604 (1990)

Supreme Court of the United States · 1990-05-29 · 89-44 published
Basis (a): decided on or after the current version

Summary (non-official) : The Court held that California’s exercise of personal jurisdiction over a nonresident personally served while physically present in California was consistent with the Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause, even though the suit was unrelated to his activities in the State. Traditional transient-presence jurisdiction remains constitutionally permissible when process is properly served.

Among the most firmly established principles of personal jurisdiction in American tradition is that the courts of a State have jurisdiction over nonresidents who are physically present in the State. The view developed early that each State had the power to hale before its courts any individual who could be found within its borders, and that once having acquired jurisdiction over such a person by properly serving him with process, the State could retain jurisdiction to enter judgment against him, no matter how fleeting his visit.
To determine whether the assertion of personal jurisdiction is consistent with due process, we have long relied on the principles traditionally followed by American courts in marking out the territorial limits of each State’s authority. That criterion was first announced in Pennoyer v. Neff, supra, in which we stated that due process “mean[s] a course of legal proceedings according to those rules and principles which have been established in our systems of jurisprudence for the protection and enforcement of private rights,” id., at 733, including the “well-established principles of public law respecting the jurisdiction of an independent State over persons and property,” id., at 722.

DeMent ex rel. DeMent v. Oglala Sioux Tribal Court, 874 F.2d 510 (8th Cir. 1989)

United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit · 1989-05-03 · 88-5105, 88-5106 published
Basis (a): decided on or after the current version

Summary (non-official) : The court applied the PKPA and held, following Thompson v. Thompson, that 28 U.S.C. § 1738A does not create an implied federal cause of action to resolve conflicting custody decisions; the claim should be raised in the subsequent custody proceeding. The court also held that exercising personal jurisdiction over a nonmember, nonresident parent in a tribal custody proceeding may violate due process under the Indian Civil Rights Act, analogizing to Kulko.

The Supreme Court has held that, in an action for child support, exercising in personam jurisdiction over a nonresident nondomiciliary parent of a minor child domiciled within a state may violate the due process under the federal Constitution. Kulko v. California Superior Court, 436 U.S. 84, 101, 98 S.Ct. 1690, 1701, 56 L.Ed.2d 132 (1978) (the Supreme Court refused to enforce a California child support decree because the father’s contacts with the state were too attenuated to satisfy traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice).
Similarly, we believe that a tribal court exercising in personam jurisdiction over a nonmember nonresident parent of a minor child domiciled within the Indian reservation may violate due process under section 1302(8) of the ICRA. We believe that DeMent may have a valid due process claim under the ICRA.

Kulko v. Superior Court, 436 U.S. 84 (1978)

Supreme Court of the United States · 1978-05-15 · 77-293 published
Basis (a): decided on or after the current version

Summary (non-official) : The Court held that California could not exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident, nondomiciliary parent in a child-support action based on his consent to his children’s relocation to California. Applying the Fourteenth Amendment’s minimum-contacts standard, the Court concluded that the father’s limited California contacts and conduct did not make it fair, just, or reasonable to require him to defend the support action there.

A father who agrees, in the interests of family harmony and his children’s preferences, to allow them to spend more time in California than was required under a separation agreement can hardly be said to have “purposefully availed himself” of the “benefits and protections” of California’s laws.
The issue before us is whether, in this action for child support, the California state courts may exercise in personam jurisdiction over a nonresident, nondomiciliary parent of minor children domiciled within the State. For reasons set forth below, we hold that the exercise of such jurisdiction would violate the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.
Like any standard that requires a determination of “reasonableness,” the “minimum contacts” test of International Shoe is not susceptible of mechanical application; rather, the facts of each case must be weighed to determine whether the requisite “affiliating circumstances” are present. Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U. S. 235, 246 (1958).

Vanderbilt v. Vanderbilt, 354 U.S. 416 (1957)

Supreme Court of the United States · 1957-06-24 · 302 published
Basis (b): identical text — functional-review

Justification : Vanderbilt interpreted U.S. Const. art. IV, § 1 and 28 U.S.C. § 1738, holding that a state court cannot adjudicate a personal claim against a nonresident absent personal jurisdiction. That jurisdictional principle is functionally consistent with the current family-law personal-jurisdiction rule reflected in Kulko, despite the absence of verbatim current text.

Summary (non-official) : The Court held that an ex parte Nevada divorce decree could dissolve the marriage but could not extinguish the wife’s support rights under New York law because Nevada lacked personal jurisdiction over her. The Full Faith and Credit Clause therefore did not require New York to recognize the decree insofar as it purported to terminate that support obligation.

Since the wife was not subject to its jurisdiction, the Nevada divorce court had no power to extinguish any right which she had under the law of New York to financial support from her husband.

It has long been the constitutional rule that a court cannot adjudicate a personal claim or obligation unless it has jurisdiction over the person of the defendant.
Art. IV, § 1. “Full Faith and Credit shall be given in each State to the public Acts, Records, and judicial Proceedings of every other State. And the Congress may by general Laws prescribe the Manner in which such Acts, Records and Proceedings shall be proved, and the Effect thereof.” Congress has provided that judgments shall have the same force and effect in every court throughout the United States that they have in the State where they were rendered. 28 U. S. C. § 1738.

May v. Anderson, 345 U.S. 528 (1953)

Supreme Court of the United States · 1953-05-18 · 244 published
Basis (a): decided on or after the current version

Summary (non-official) : The Court held that Ohio was not required to give full faith and credit to the Wisconsin custody award because Wisconsin lacked personal jurisdiction over the mother, who was neither domiciled, resident, nor present there and did not appear. Although the ex parte divorce could be recognized as dissolving the marriage, the custody component could not bind the nonresident mother.

The question presented is whether, in a habeas corpus proceeding attacking the right of a mother to retain possession of her minor children, an Ohio court must give full faith and credit to a Wisconsin decree awarding custody of the children to their father when that decree is obtained by the father in an ex parte divorce action in a Wisconsin court which had no personal jurisdiction over the mother. For the reasons hereafter stated, our answer is no.
Separated as our issue is from that of the future interests of the children, we have before us the elemental question whether a court of a state, where a mother is neither domiciled, resident nor present, may cut off her immediate right to the care, custody, management and companionship of her minor children without having jurisdiction over her in personam. Rights far more precious to appellant than property rights will be cut off if she is to be bound by the Wisconsin award of custody.
I]t is now too well settled to be open to further dispute that the 'full faith and credit’ clause and the act of Congress passed pursuant to it do not entitle a judgment in personam to extra-territorial effect if it be made to appear that it was rendered without jurisdiction over the person sought to be bound.” Baker v. Baker, Eccles & Co., 242 U. S. 394, 401, and see 403; Thompson v. Whitman, 18 Wall. 457; D’Arcy v. Ketchum, 11 How. 165.
