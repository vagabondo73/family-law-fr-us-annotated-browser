Lucas Alzu v. Amy Huff, No. 24-2517 (8th Cir. 2026)

United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit · 2026-01-23 · 24-2517 published
Basis (a): decided on or after the current version

Summary (non-official) : The court applied ICARA and Hague Convention principles governing wrongful removal, holding that the petitioner bears the burden of proving wrongful removal or retention. Applying Monasky’s totality-of-the-circumstances, fact-driven standard, the court affirmed the finding that Argentina was not the child’s habitual residence.

The determination of a child’s habitual residence is a mixed question of law and fact. Monasky, 589 U.S. at 84. “Once the trial court correctly identifies the governing totality-of-the-circumstances standard, . . . [t]he habitual residence determination . . . should be judged on appeal by a clear-error review standard deferential to the factfinding court.” Id.; See also Tsuruta v. Tsuruta, 76 F.4th 1107, 1111 (8th Cir. 2023) (same).
The petitioner bears the burden of proving ‘that the child has been wrongfully removed or retained within the meaning of the Convention.’” Id. at 917, citing 42 U.S.C. § 11603(e)(1)(A).
L]ocating a child’s home is a fact-driven inquiry[.] [C]ourts must be ‘sensitive to the unique circumstances of the case and informed by common sense.

Naoteru Tsuruta v. Sarah Tsuruta, 76 F.4th 1107 (8th Cir. 2023)

United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit · 2023-08-10 · 22-3014 published
Basis (a): decided on or after the current version

Summary (non-official) : The court applied the Hague Convention’s wrongful-removal framework and held that Japan was the child’s habitual residence before removal. Habitual residence is determined by where the child was “at home” based on the totality of the circumstances, and does not require an actual parental agreement.

The Hague Convention does not govern custody battles, instead the Hague Convention determines the proper forum for custody battles. Id. at 916–17. Under the Hague Convention, “a child wrongfully removed from her country of ‘habitual residence’ ordinarily must be returned to that country[]” where custody will be determined. Monasky v. Taglieri, 140 S. Ct. 719, 723 (2020).
A child’s habitual residence is “[t]he place where a child is at home, at the time of removal or retention[.]” Monasky, 140 S. Ct. at 726. An actual agreement between the parents is not required to establish habitual residence, instead “a child’s habitual residence depends on the totality of the circumstances specific to the case.” Monasky, 140 S. Ct. at 723.

Monasky v. Taglieri, 589 U.S. 68 (2020)

Supreme Court of the United States · 2020-02-25 · 18-935 published
Basis (a): decided on or after the current version

Summary (non-official) : The Court held that a child’s habitual residence under the Hague Convention is determined by the totality of the circumstances specific to the case, rather than by categorical requirements such as an actual parental agreement. It also held that a trial court’s habitual-residence determination is a fact-intensive mixed question subject to clear-error review on appeal.

A child’s habitual residence depends on the totality of the circumstances specific to the case
Held: 1. A child’s habitual residence depends on the totality of the circumstances specific to the case, not on categorical requirements such as an actual agreement between the parents. Pp. 7–14. (a) The inquiry begins with the Convention’s text “and the context 2 MONASKY v. TAGLIERI Syllabus in which the written words are used.
In accord with decisions of the courts of other countries party to the Convention, we hold that a child’s habitual residence depends on the totality of the circumstances specific to the case. An actual agreement between the parents is not necessary to establish an infant’s habitual residence. We further hold that a first-instance habitualresidence determination is subject to deferential appellate review for clear error.

Stern v. Stern, 639 F.3d 449 (8th Cir. 2011)

United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit · 2011-05-06 · 10-2493 published
Basis (b): identical text — functional-review

Justification : Stern interpreted ICARA, 22 U.S.C. § 9003, and the Hague Convention’s habitual-residence requirement through a fact-intensive assessment of the child’s connections to the competing countries. Its analysis—contrasting DJ’s limited connections with Israel against his substantial, longstanding connections to Iowa—is consistent in function with Monasky’s totality-of-the-circumstances formulation, even though Stern predates and does not use Monasky’s exact wording.

Summary (non-official) : The court applied ICARA to determine whether DJ had been wrongfully retained in the United States. It held that the United States was DJ’s habitual residence, so Martin was not entitled to an ICARA remedy requiring his return to Israel for custody adjudication.

Martin brought this action in the district court petitioning for DJ’s return to Israel under ICARA. That act implements the Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction, 19 I.L.M. 1501 (1980) (Convention). ICARA does not control substantive custody disputes, but rather governs selection of the forum where such a dispute should be brought. Barzilay v. Barzilay, 600 F.3d 912, 916 (8th Cir.2010). The key question under ICARA is “whether a child has been wrongfully removed from the country of its habitual residence or wrongfully retained in a country other than that of its habitual residence.
The district court held a bench trial in June 2010, after which it found that DJ habitually resided in the United States. The court found “little evidence” about DJ’s connections with Israel. In contrast, it found DJ had “considerable connections with his current [Iowa] environment.” At the time DJ was seven years old and had been living in Iowa since he was two.

Justification : Sorenson interpreted Hague Convention Article 3’s habitual-residence requirement, as implemented by ICARA, through a fact-specific inquiry into the child’s circumstances and concluded that Australia was the habitual residence. Because we find that Australia was the child’s habitual residence at the time of the allegedly wrongful retention, we affirm the decision of the district court.

It also held that Rooker-Feldman did not bar the federal Hague proceeding because the state court had not decided the Convention-specific issues of habitual residence or wrongful removal and retention.

Because we find that the children’s habitual residence was Israel at the time of their removal and that there is no grave risk of harm to the children if they are returned to Israel, we reverse the district court.

Silverman v. Silverman, 312 F.3d 914 (8th Cir. 2002)

United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit · 2002-12-11 · 02-2496 published
Basis (b): identical text — functional-review

Justification : Silverman interpreted Hague Convention Article 3 and 42 U.S.C. § 11603(e)(1)(A), holding that habitual residence is a fact-based, case-by-case inquiry. That approach is consistent in wording and function with Monasky’s current totality-of-the-circumstances rule, even though the quoted language does not appear verbatim in the current text.

Summary (non-official) : The court held that habitual residence under the Hague Convention and ICARA is a purely factual, case-specific inquiry, reviewed for clear error. The parent seeking return bears the preponderance-of-the-evidence burden to show that the children were removed from their habitual residence; because the children’s habitual residence was the United States, the court did not reach the Article 13(b) defense.
