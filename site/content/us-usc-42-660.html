42 U.S.C. § 660 — Civil action to enforce child support obligations; jurisdiction of district courts

in forceIn force since : 1984-07-18 · Applicable : US

Official source : https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title42-section660&num=0&edition=prelim · govinfo (USCODE) · Interactive browser

Current text

The district courts of the United States shall have jurisdiction, without regard to any amount in controversy, to hear and determine any civil action certified by the Secretary of Health and Human Services under section 652(a)(8) of this title. A civil action under this section may be brought in any judicial district in which the claim arose, the plaintiff resides, or the defendant resides.

Binding interpretations (2)

Schweiss v. Chrysler Motors Corp., 922 F.2d 473 (8th Cir. 1990)

United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit · 1990-12-31 · 89-2925 published
Basis (a): decided on or after the current version

Summary (non-official) : The court rejected Chrysler’s argument that 42 U.S.C. § 660(c) preempted state-law claims providing the asserted relief. It found no such preemptive intent in the statutory text or legislative history.

Nothing in the text of the statute shows such an intent. Further, Chrysler does not point us to any suggestion in the legislative history of 42 U.S.C. § 660(c) that Congress intended to preempt state law actions that allow such relief.

Overman v. United States, 563 F.2d 1287 (8th Cir. 1977)

United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit · 1977-10-25 · 76-1997 published
Basis (b): identical text — functional-review

Justification : Overman interpreted 42 U.S.C. § 660’s grant of federal jurisdiction for civil actions arising under § 652(a)(8) when certified by the Secretary, and the current text retains that same jurisdictional mechanism and venue provisions. The 1984 amendment only changed the agency name from Health, Education, and Welfare to Health and Human Services, a technical change that does not alter the provision’s wording in substance or function.

Summary (non-official) : The court held that 42 U.S.C. § 659 waived sovereign immunity only for state garnishment proceedings enforcing child-support or alimony obligations, and did not authorize a broader damages or validity challenge against the United States or its disbursing officer. It further reasoned that § 660's expressly certified jurisdictional mechanism did not extend to § 659 actions, and remanded the remaining domestic-relations dispute to state court under the federal policy against federal adjudication of such matters.

In 42 U.S.C. § 659 (Supp.1975), the United States waived its immunity from state garnishment actions directed at federal employees. The statute simply removed the bar of sovereign immunity to one narrow class of actions: enforcement of garnishment writs issued by state courts. See, e. g., Wilhelm v. United States Department of Air Force Accounting, 418 F.Supp. 162, 164 (S.D.Tex.1976). Nothing in the statute or legislative history, however, indicates any congressional intent to expose the Government to wider liability.
Congressman Ullman’s comment about certification is a reference to 42 U.S.C. § 660 (Supp. 1975). That provision provides federal jurisdiction for civil actions arising under 42 U.S.C. § 652(a)(8) (Supp. 1975), if certified by the Secretary of Health, Education and Welfare. Since §§ 652(a)(8) and 659 are parallel provisions, that is, both are part of Pub.L. 93-647, § 606(a), 88 Stat. 2351, 2357, the presumption must be that if Congress had intended federal jurisdiction for cases arising under § 659 it would have explicitly provided for it, as it did for § 652(a)(8).
